# How species replace themselves: the real world and the terrarium (season 2427, Oct 21)

*Written by the world steward, Claude (Opus 5.5), an AI, at Luis's request. Not written by Luis.*

**The question (Luis):** new species split off from the quilikes but keep dying out. How do reproduction cycles come about in the real world, through size, metabolism, diet and heartbeats, and what does that say about the terrarium?

## 1. The real world: life history

**Metabolic scaling (the "¼-power laws"):** across animals, many rates follow body mass M:

| Measure | Scales as | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Metabolic rate (energy per day) | M^¾ (Kleiber) | big animals burn more in total, but less per kilogram |
| Heart rate | M^−¼ | a mouse ~600 beats a minute, an elephant ~30 |
| Lifespan, age at maturity, generation time | M^¼ | small animals live fast and die young |
| Heartbeats per lifetime | ≈ constant | ~1–1.5 billion for most mammals: a lifetime is about the same number of beats |
| Maximum population growth rate | M^−¼ | small species bounce back fast, big ones slowly |

**Life-history strategies (r and K):**
- **Small, fast species** mature early, have many young per year, and invest little in each. They survive by numbers.
- **Large, slow species** mature late, have few young, invest heavily (care, protection, large newborns), and survive by quality.

**Diet matters:**
- Plant-eaters live at high densities and can afford many young.
- Meat-eaters live at low densities (the ~10% energy pyramid), reproduce more slowly, and invest more in each young.
- Insect-eaters are usually small and fast.

**Small populations have extra problems (the Allee effect):**
- **Finding mates:** rare animals meet each other less often. Nature answers with signals that carry far (song, scent, display), gatherings (herds, colonies, leks where males gather to display, spawning grounds), and fixed breeding seasons.
- **Predation:** a few young are easy to pick off. Nature answers with synchronous births (so many young at once that predators can't eat them all: "predator swamping"), hiding the young, group defence and vigilance, and parental care.

## 2. The terrarium today

| Real world | Terrarium rule | Match? |
|---|---|---|
| Heartbeats set a life | Age is counted in **heartbeats**; heart rate ∝ (mass)^−¼; maturity, lifespan and refractory are measured in heartbeats | **Yes.** Small bodies already live faster in ticks |
| Metabolism ∝ M^¾ | Upkeep ∝ scale^0.75 (Kleiber) | Yes |
| Investment per young | Parents give a share of their energy (the "gift") plus tissue (B); litter size is a gene, and young split the gift | Partly. Litter and gift aren't tied to size or diet, and evolve freely |
| Finding mates | Two willing bodies must be within **~4.5 units** (after the footprint change); choosiness relaxes with waiting; after 600 ticks alone, a well-fed body may **bud** alone (losing 70% of the gift) | **The weak point for rare species** |
| Breeding seasons, synchrony | None: bodies breed whenever willing, fertile and rested | Missing |
| Group defence, vigilance, hiding the young | Hiding in thicket (small bodies), fleeing, armour, toxin, grace season | Partly. No group defence |
| Aggregation (herds, leks) | None until **flock** (being tested) | Coming |

**Two failures, in this order:**
1. **Predation.** New species lose more to being eaten than to hunger. 127 eaten against 189 born for ushsasa. They're small groups among ~200 quilikes, and fair game once their grace ends.
2. **Mate finding.** Their bodies sit 26–37 units from the nearest kin, but mating needs ~4.5. They breed far less often than their numbers would allow. Budding alone almost never happens, because it needs a long wait while well fed.

## 4. What could help (for Luis to decide; flock is already being tested)

| Option | Real-world model | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Flock** (testing now) | herds, family groups | Kin of a small species gather in groups of 6–8. Mates are near, and groups can defend themselves (with D) |
| **B. Calls: willing bodies find each other** | song, scent, display | A willing body senses willing kin from its whole sight range (not only 4.5 units) and steers toward them. Stronger for small species |
| **C. Breeding season** | spawning, rut, birth pulses | Births cluster in the warm, wet part of each season, so predators are swamped and young arrive when food is richest |
| **D. Safety in numbers** | vigilance, mobbing | A body in a group of kin is harder to catch (each extra neighbour lowers the hunter's chance a little, up to a cap) |
| E. Young stay with a parent | parental care | Newborns follow a parent for their first part of life and are protected while they do |
| F. Life history by size | r/K strategies | Litter size and gift tied to body size: small bodies have more, smaller young |

**Steward's recommendation:**
- Finish testing **A**. It addresses both failures a little.
- Then **B**, the cheapest fix for mate finding.
- Then **D**, the direct answer to predation.
- **C** is the most "natural" and could come after, if births still don't keep up.
- **E and F** are bigger changes, for later.
